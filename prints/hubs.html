Where hubs go

Hubs go where houses come down and multiplexes go up, on land a workshop could use beside a truck route. Four stated rules place six sites, and six is roughly what the city's pace of demolition would keep busy.

Key

  • house demolitions, 2017–2026
  • new multiplexes, since June 2024
  • land a workshop could use, by a truck route
  • hub site and 1.5 km ring, about a 20-minute walk

The answer, in brief

What this is not

Candidates produced by rules, not selected properties. No parcel, owner, price or lot size has been checked, and every site would still need a rezoning or a conditional approval. The capacity figures behind the number six are hypotheses to test.

How the six were found: four rules

A hub takes in houses as large panels and offers their parts to the next buildings. So it should sit where houses come down and homes are being built, on land that can hold a workshop and a public building, by a road trucks may use.

Who and what each ring holds

Why six

hubs = share of houses taken × houses demolished a year ÷ houses one hub can take apart a year

  • One hub's year. Cornell's Circular Construction Lab took one Ithaca house apart in 447.5 labour hours on site and 96 off site. I assume a hub moves about two thirds of the total, 360 hours, off site. A crew of 12 at 1,700 productive hours each then takes apart about 57 houses a year, about one a week.
  • Share. Half the houses, as a working target: some will still be demolished, and some taken apart on site by firms that already do it.

A week of house demolitions; orange, what six hubs could take apart.

How far six moves, when each assumption changes

Hubs needed, by share of houses taken (rows), crew size and off-site hours a house (columns); base case highlighted. 678 houses a year, 1,700 hours a worker.

Reach, as hubs are added in order

Each hub brings less than the one before, but not sharply less: the curve cannot pick the number, so the work a hub can do picks it. Building one or two first would test these figures.

What a hub also offers its neighbours

A multiplex turns one household into as many as eight, but adds no shared room. Each hub could add rooms along its street, and teach the work it does. First thoughts on the programme, not a layout.

  • café and grocery daily visits
  • plaza and court open space
  • culture and exhibition the parts' stories
  • a classroom learning by watching the work
  • gather, analyse, unbuild, sort, offer the deconstruction itself

Limits

  • Straight lines, not road routes; real hauls are longer.
  • Permits read from free text, so counts are a floor; a permit records a demolition, not what was recovered.
  • Households from 2021, spread evenly over each dissemination area; the multiplexes since are not counted.
  • The capacity figures are mine, resting on one house in Ithaca, not on Vancouver practice.
  • Zoning is a proxy, not a permitted-use check; C‑2 lots are shallow, so a hub there needs lots assembled or City land.
  • Equal weight to houses and multiplexes; weighting supply would pull the chain east.

What comes next

Sources and method

City of Vancouver open data, fetched 24–26 Oct 2026: building permits (demolition since 2017; new buildings naming a multiplex since June 2024), zoning, truck routes, community centres, rapid transit, City-owned properties, local areas. Statistics Canada, 2021 Census by dissemination area. Heisel et al., Cornell Circular Construction Lab, 2023. Method: build/hub_siting.py; candidate cells of 50 m; households on a 10 m grid; greedy maximum coverage within 1.5 km, houses and multiplexes as equal shares.
